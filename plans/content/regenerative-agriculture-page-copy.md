## Regenerative Agriculture Is a Production System and a Value-Chain Program

Regenerative agriculture begins with how crops are produced, but it does not
scale through agronomy alone.

Farmers decide what can work in a particular field and production system.
Originators and processors connect producers to physical markets. Food,
beverage, feed, fiber, and energy companies create demand and determine what
evidence they need. Technology providers collect and analyze data. Accounting,
certification, environmental-market, and assurance frameworks determine what
can be reported or claimed.

A successful program must connect all of those decisions without losing sight
of the farm business that makes implementation possible.

Terra Nexus works across the complete system so regenerative agriculture can
move from a broad ambition to a commercially relevant, operationally
executable, measurable, and credible program.

## What Regenerative Agriculture Means in Practice

There is no single practice list or universally accepted definition that fits
every crop, geography, farm, buyer, or market pathway.

Leading frameworks increasingly treat regenerative agriculture as a
context-specific, outcome-oriented approach. The objective is to improve the
function and resilience of agricultural systems while maintaining or improving
the livelihoods and productive capacity of the people who manage them.

Depending on the context, desired outcomes may include:

### Healthier and More Functional Soils

- improved soil structure and biological activity;
- greater protection from erosion;
- improved nutrient cycling;
- more effective water infiltration and retention;
- maintenance or improvement of soil organic matter and soil carbon.

### More Resilient Crop Production

- greater ability to manage drought, excess water, heat, and other production risks;
- more diverse rotations and biological functions;
- improved input efficiency;
- more stable productivity and quality over time;
- reduced exposure to selected operating costs or supply disruptions.

### Better Water and Biodiversity Outcomes

- reduced runoff, erosion, and nutrient loss;
- improved water-use efficiency;
- increased plant, soil, pollinator, and habitat diversity;
- stronger ecological function within and around working lands.

### Stronger Farm and Supply-Chain Economics

- transition support that reflects producer cost and risk;
- potential input, yield, quality, or resilience benefits;
- access to customer programs, differentiated markets, or incentives;
- improved long-term supply security for buyers;
- more durable relationships among producers, originators, and downstream customers.

Potential practices may include cover crops, reduced soil disturbance, diverse
rotations, nutrient and water management, residue management, integrated
livestock, perennial systems, habitat features, and other locally appropriate
changes. These are examples—not a universal prescription or a guarantee that a
practice will create the same outcome everywhere.

## Why Program Design Matters as Much as Practice Selection

A list of approved practices does not create a scalable regenerative agriculture
program.

The commercial and operating model must answer:

- What business objective is the program intended to serve?
- Which crops, geographies, supply sheds, and producers are relevant?
- Which outcomes matter in the local context?
- Which changes are practical for participating farms?
- Who funds transition costs and carries performance risk?
- What data and evidence are required?
- How will physical commodities and environmental attributes move?
- Which claims, reports, or market mechanisms may be supported?
- Who owns and may use each resulting attribute or claim?
- How will the program be managed, reviewed, and improved over time?

Programs often struggle when these questions are addressed separately or too
late. Terra Nexus helps clients design them as one connected system.

## How a Regenerative Agriculture Program Moves from Concept to Scale

### 1. Define the Objective and Program Boundary

Start with the business decision rather than the practice list.

The objective may be to strengthen a strategic supply shed, reduce agricultural
emissions, improve water or soil outcomes, create a differentiated product,
qualify suppliers, support corporate targets, access an incentive, or build a
new environmental-market offering.

Terra Nexus helps define the crops, products, geographies, participants,
reporting boundary, commercial objective, and intended users of the outcome.

### 2. Understand the Local Production and Market Context

Regenerative agriculture must fit the production system.

The relevant context includes soil, climate, crop rotation, water, current
management, equipment, tenancy, agronomic risk, producer economics, local
advisors, infrastructure, commodity flows, buyer requirements, and available
programs or incentives.

Terra Nexus combines field and supply-shed context with customer demand and
commercial feasibility so clients can prioritize where a program has a credible
path to adoption and value.

### 3. Design the Producer Proposition

Producers need a clear reason and practical path to participate.

A producer offer may include technical support, cost sharing, premiums,
outcome payments, multi-year commitments, access to markets, technology,
financing, risk-sharing mechanisms, or combinations of these.

Terra Nexus helps clients model transition costs, value allocation, enrollment
criteria, contracting, evidence requirements, timing, and the treatment of
existing adopters, new adopters, tenants, landowners, and incomplete data.

### 4. Build Evidence, Data, and Traceability Around the Intended Use

The evidence system should be designed for the decision or claim it must
support—not assembled after implementation.

Depending on the program, evidence may include producer attestations, field
records, input and practice data, soil samples, remote sensing, models,
transaction records, chain-of-custody controls, product allocation, calculation
workbooks, quality checks, and independent assurance.

Terra Nexus helps determine:

- what must be measured directly;
- what may be modeled or remotely sensed;
- what must be traceable to field, farm, supplier, or supply shed;
- how uncertainty, missing information, updates, and exceptions are handled;
- how physical products and environmental attributes are allocated;
- what documentation must be retained for customers, accounting, certification,
  crediting, or verification.

### 5. Launch, Operate, and Improve the Program

Commercial-scale programs require recurring operations—not only a methodology
and a software platform.

Core activities may include producer outreach, enrollment, contract management,
data collection, quality control, calculation, ledger management, supplier and
buyer reconciliation, partner coordination, exception handling, reporting,
verification readiness, and continuous improvement.

Terra Nexus can advise on the operating model or manage defined workstreams
within a client-owned program.

### 6. Connect Improved Performance to the Right Form of Value

The same field-level improvement may support different forms of value for
different participants.

Potential pathways include:

- regenerative sourcing or program-participation claims;
- certification and product differentiation;
- Scope 3 reductions and insets;
- carbon or other ecosystem-service credits;
- low-carbon feedstock or regulatory value;
- procurement qualification and customer reporting;
- resilience, supply-security, and risk-reduction value.

These pathways have different evidence, traceability, ownership, assurance,
commercial reach, and cost requirements. Some may coexist; others may require
exclusive allocation or careful claims controls.

Terra Nexus helps clients compare the realizable value, cost, risk, timing,
utilization, market capacity, and strategic benefit of each pathway. The goal is
not simply the highest nominal price. It is the highest-value supportable play
across the participants required to deliver it.

## Commodity Traders and Originators Are Often Central to Scale

Commodity traders and originators frequently sit between farm-level change and
downstream demand.

They may hold the producer relationships, local market knowledge, physical
origination capability, aggregation infrastructure, contracts, logistics,
customer relationships, and data needed to connect production with market
value.

Terra Nexus helps traders and originators:

- design regenerative commodity and environmental-attribute offerings;
- structure producer enrollment, incentives, evidence, and contracts;
- establish origination, aggregation, traceability, and chain-of-custody controls;
- model premiums, program costs, value sharing, and margin;
- select technology, agronomy, MRV, and assurance partners;
- manage incomplete, conflicting, duplicated, or non-verifiable data;
- prepare customer documentation, reporting, and audit packages;
- scale programs across producers, locations, crops, and buyers.

## Decisions Clients Need to Resolve

### Strategy and Commercial Leaders

- Which regenerative opportunities fit the business strategy and customer demand?
- Which crops, supply sheds, products, and outcomes offer a credible business case?
- Should the company build, buy, partner, invest, or participate?
- How should value, transition costs, and risk be shared?

### Origination, Procurement, and Operations

- Which producers and suppliers are ready to participate?
- How should volumes be sourced, aggregated, contracted, and reconciled?
- How should existing adopters and incomplete traceability be treated?
- What evidence must move with the commodity or environmental attribute?

### Sustainability, Climate, and Claims Teams

- Is the program intended for inventory progress, Scope 3, insetting,
  certification, product claims, carbon credits, resilience, water, biodiversity,
  or a combination?
- Which accounting, target, certification, or market framework applies?
- How should reductions, removals, avoided emissions, and broader outcomes be separated?
- What may be reported credibly, by whom, and at what level of assurance?

### Program, Technology, and Data Teams

- What data must be collected directly and what may be modeled?
- Which systems, vendors, models, field evidence, and ledgers must connect?
- How will data quality, versioning, corrections, exceptions, and access be managed?
- Which activities belong internally, with partners, or in a managed service?

### Finance, Investment, and Risk Teams

- What is the expected cost and value per acre, tonne, supplier, or product unit?
- How sensitive is the business case to participation, outcomes, market demand, and assurance cost?
- What methodology, counterparty, operational, fraud, and claims risks exist?
- Is the program, platform, project, or portfolio investment supportable?

## Common Reasons Regenerative Programs Struggle

- The program starts with a practice list rather than a clear business objective.
- Producer costs, risk, timing, and operational burden are underestimated.
- A technology platform is selected before the operating model and evidence requirements are defined.
- Models, field data, contracts, commodity flows, and claims use different boundaries.
- Traceability is not sufficient for the intended accounting or product claim.
- Environmental attributes and usage rights are unclear or allocated more than once.
- The customer proposition does not cover the cost of origination, data, assurance, and management.
- Pilot success criteria do not test the assumptions required for commercial scale.
- Ongoing program management, corrections, exceptions, and verification readiness are not adequately resourced.

Terra Nexus helps clients identify these gaps before they become expensive
operating or claims problems.
