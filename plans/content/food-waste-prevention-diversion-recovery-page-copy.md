## Food Waste Is a Portfolio of Business Problems

Food loss and waste can occur before harvest, during storage and transportation,
in processing, in manufacturing, at retail, in foodservice, or after purchase.
The causes and solutions are different at every stage.

A forecasting problem should not be treated like a processing byproduct. An
edible surplus should not be managed like contaminated material. A valuable
ingredient stream should not be sent to a lower-value recovery pathway because
the market, logistics, or quality requirements were never developed.

The most effective approach is to understand where food, nutrients, materials,
energy, labor, and money are being lost and then prioritize the highest practical
use.

Terra Nexus helps clients treat food waste as an integrated portfolio of
prevention, redistribution, product, operational, market, and recovery
opportunities rather than a single disposal problem.

## What Food Waste Prevention, Diversion, and Recovery Includes

This Area of Expertise covers the strategies, operating systems, markets,
technologies, and partnerships used to keep food and related resources in their
highest practical use.

It may include:

### Prevention

Demand planning, forecasting, inventory, procurement, production scheduling,
process control, quality, yield, shelf life, packaging, product design, handling,
storage, transportation, merchandising, and customer behavior.

### Edible-Food Redistribution

Surplus sales, secondary markets, donation, rescue, foodservice and retail
programs, logistics, safety, quality, liability, and coordination with community
partners.

### Upcycling and Product Development

Creation of ingredients, food, feed, beverages, biomaterials, or other products
from byproducts, surplus, side streams, or underutilized materials.

### Animal Feed and Agricultural Uses

Use of suitable materials in feed, soil amendments, fertilizers, compost,
agricultural inputs, or other productive pathways subject to quality, safety,
regulatory, and market requirements.

### Organics Recycling and Biological Recovery

Composting, anaerobic digestion, nutrient recovery, wastewater and organic-
material management, and other biological treatment systems.

### Energy and Resource Recovery

Recovery of energy, fuels, heat, water, nutrients, materials, or other value from
remaining streams when higher-value pathways are not practical.

### Measurement, Reporting, and Claims

Food-loss and waste accounting, material-flow analysis, baselines, targets,
diversion, emissions, circularity, avoided cost, new revenue, and claims.

The page should reflect a hierarchy of value: prevent avoidable loss first, keep
edible food in use, create higher-value products and markets, use appropriate
feed or agricultural pathways, recover nutrients and energy, and reserve
disposal for material that has no practical higher use.

## What Better Performance Can Look Like

Potential improvement categories include:

### Lower Cost and Better Yield

- reduced raw-material loss, giveaway, overproduction, spoilage, and rework;
- improved process yield, inventory turns, and shelf life;
- lower hauling, treatment, disposal, and compliance cost;
- improved labor, energy, water, packaging, and asset utilization;
- fewer quality failures and write-offs.

### More Food Kept in Use

- better matching of supply and demand;
- earlier identification of surplus;
- stronger donation, rescue, markdown, and secondary-market pathways;
- improved logistics and cold-chain coordination;
- reduced loss across farms, processors, distribution, retail, and foodservice.

### New Revenue and Product Value

- sale of co-products and byproducts;
- new ingredients, feedstocks, food products, feed, materials, or services;
- improved valuation of side streams;
- new customer and channel relationships;
- licensing, partnership, or venture opportunities.

### Improved Environmental Performance

- reduced embedded land, water, energy, and emissions associated with wasted food;
- lower disposal and treatment impacts;
- recovery of nutrients, energy, and materials;
- improved circularity and resource efficiency;
- stronger corporate target and reporting performance where supported by evidence.

### Greater Resilience and Collaboration

- more flexible outlets for surplus and byproducts;
- stronger coordination among suppliers, operations, customers, recovery partners,
  community organizations, and regulators;
- reduced exposure to disposal capacity, commodity volatility, and changing rules;
- better visibility into material flows and root causes.

These outcomes depend on the material, quality, geography, timing, regulation,
customer demand, logistics, infrastructure, and operating environment.

## Why Prevention Usually Creates the Most Value

Once food or a byproduct leaves its intended use, part of its value has often
already been lost.

The business has paid for raw materials, production, labor, energy, water,
packaging, storage, transportation, and management. A lower-value recovery
pathway may reduce disposal cost or create revenue, but it rarely recovers the
full value embedded in the original product.

Prevention may involve:

- improving forecasting and planning;
- changing specifications or procurement practices;
- reducing production variability;
- improving equipment, yield, quality, or maintenance;
- redesigning products or packaging;
- extending shelf life;
- changing merchandising, ordering, or customer practices;
- improving data, accountability, and incentives.

Terra Nexus helps clients compare prevention with downstream pathways using the
complete economics rather than only the disposal fee or revenue per tonne.

## How a Food-Waste Opportunity Moves from Material Loss to Value

### 1. Define the Objective and Scope

The starting point may be cost reduction, waste targets, operational excellence,
new revenue, a byproduct challenge, regulatory change, food donation, circularity,
facility investment, or a new technology.

Terra Nexus helps define the facilities, products, materials, geographies,
participants, data, commercial objective, and decision boundary.

### 2. Measure Material and Value Flows

A useful assessment shows where material comes from, why it is lost, what quality
it retains, where it goes, what it costs, and who controls the decision.

Relevant information may include:

- material type, quantity, composition, and variability;
- edible versus inedible portions;
- quality, safety, contamination, and shelf life;
- process, product, line, shift, location, supplier, and customer;
- seasonality and timing;
- handling, storage, and transportation;
- current outlet, treatment, cost, revenue, and contractual terms;
- embedded raw-material and processing value;
- root causes, ownership, and decision rights.

Terra Nexus helps create a material-and-value baseline that supports operational,
commercial, investment, and reporting decisions.

### 3. Prioritize Prevention and Highest-Value Pathways

Opportunities should be compared using more than environmental preference.
Relevant factors include:

- avoidable cost and recoverable value;
- food safety, quality, and legal requirements;
- customer and market demand;
- material consistency and scale;
- logistics and infrastructure;
- capital and operating requirements;
- partner capability and counterparty risk;
- emissions and environmental performance;
- implementation timing and scalability;
- organizational ownership and change required.

Terra Nexus helps clients build a prioritized portfolio rather than forcing every
material into one technology or outlet.

### 4. Develop the Business Case and Market

A potential byproduct or surplus market must work for both the generator and the
buyer.

Terra Nexus helps evaluate:

- product or feedstock specifications;
- processing, stabilization, testing, packaging, and storage;
- volume, seasonality, and reliability;
- customer applications and alternatives;
- pricing, avoided cost, revenue, margin, and logistics;
- capital, working capital, and payback;
- contractual responsibilities, liability, and quality;
- ownership of claims, credits, or environmental attributes;
- partner, customer, and route-to-market strategy.

### 5. Select Technology and Partners Around the Operating Need

Technology may support forecasting, measurement, resale, donation, separation,
processing, upcycling, composting, digestion, treatment, or resource recovery.

Terra Nexus helps clients evaluate technology readiness, feedstock fit,
integration, performance claims, utilities, maintenance, permitting, output
markets, vendor quality, financing, and scale.

Technology is selected after the material, operating, and commercial problem is
clear—not as the starting point.

### 6. Build and Launch the Operating Model

Implementation may require new procedures, segregation, quality controls,
inventory rules, training, equipment, data capture, contracts, logistics,
customer development, partner coordination, and reporting.

Terra Nexus helps design roles, workflows, controls, decision rights, pilots,
implementation plans, and scale gates. We can also manage recurring workstreams
within client-owned programs.

### 7. Measure Financial, Operational, and Environmental Performance

Programs should track the outcomes relevant to the decision, which may include:

- tonnes prevented, redistributed, repurposed, recycled, or recovered;
- product and process yield;
- cost avoided and revenue created;
- edible food kept in use;
- material quality and outlet reliability;
- emissions, energy, water, nutrients, or landfill diversion;
- customer, community, and partner outcomes;
- capital performance and operating reliability.

Terra Nexus helps align measurement, accounting, claims, and management review so
reported results match the actual operating system.

## Decisions Clients Need to Resolve

- Where is food or material value being lost and why?
- Which losses can be prevented rather than managed downstream?
- What is edible, safe, stable, and commercially usable?
- Which outlet preserves the highest practical value?
- Is the material consistent enough to support a customer or technology?
- What quality, safety, legal, logistics, and liability requirements apply?
- What are the complete economics, including embedded value and avoided cost?
- Which technologies and partners fit the operating environment?
- Who owns the material, output, claim, and environmental attribute?
- How will the program scale without shifting cost or impact elsewhere?

## Common Reasons Food-Waste Programs Struggle

- the company begins with disposal rather than root causes and prevention;
- total embedded product value is ignored in favor of hauling cost;
- materials are grouped together despite different quality and market potential;
- technology is selected before feedstock and output markets are validated;
- expected volume is inconsistent, seasonal, contaminated, or unavailable;
- food safety, liability, quality, and permitting are addressed too late;
- logistics consume the value of the proposed pathway;
- the buyer and generator have mismatched specifications or incentives;
- waste and circularity claims use inconsistent boundaries;
- pilots launch without recurring ownership, data, or performance management.

Terra Nexus helps clients identify these gaps and build a portfolio that protects
value before material reaches a lower-use pathway.
