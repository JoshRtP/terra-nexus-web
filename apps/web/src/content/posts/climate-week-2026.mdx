Climate Week schedules have a shape to them. Panels in the morning, a report launch over lunch, a rooftop reception in the evening where everyone holds a drink they are not drinking and asks each other what brings you here. Repeat for seven days. By Thursday you have collected forty business cards and had roughly two conversations you actually remember.

I have done a lot of those weeks. They are genuinely useful — this is where deals start and where you find out what people are really working on. But the format does something strange to the food, ag, and land part of this work specifically, and it took me a while to name it.

## This work runs on trust that takes years to build

The projects I spend my time on do not close in a quarter. A producer deciding to change a practice is making a multi-year bet on a buyer who might not be there in year three. A program that needs field-level data is asking people to hand over records they have no particular reason to share. Verification, certification, offtake, the whole apparatus — none of it works without someone on the other end deciding you are worth believing.

You do not build that standing at a high-top table for eleven minutes.

<Callout variant="insight" title="The thing about small talk">
  Networking formats are optimized for volume — meet as many people as possible, as briefly as possible. The relationships this sector actually runs on are the opposite: few people, over a long time, in situations where it is obvious what kind of person you are.
</Callout>

## So this year, a field day

During Climate Week I will be on a lawn in Central Park at eight in the morning, doing something closer to a field day than a conference session. Bagels and coffee first. Then an hour of games that range from mildly competitive to completely ridiculous. Then awards, and whatever conversations happen after.

It is the first annual NYCW Food, Ag, Land Field Day, presented by Farmhand Ventures and co-organized by Carbon A List. I am going along with Connie Bowen, Christophe Jospe, Sami Tellatin, Kevin Silverman, and a number of other people who work in this space and have also, I suspect, had enough of the rooftop.

Here is the honest case for it: you learn more about someone in one round of a stupid outdoor game than in four rooftop conversations. Who competes hard and stays generous about it. Who organizes the rest of the team without being asked. Who is still cheerful when their side is losing. Those turn out to be extremely good predictors of what a person is like to work with on a program that will take three years and go sideways at least twice.

## If you are going to be in New York

Come. Registration is through the event page, and the hosts approve requests, so it is worth doing early rather than the night before.

Bring sunscreen and a competitive spirit.
